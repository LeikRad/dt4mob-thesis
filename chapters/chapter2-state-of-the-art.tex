\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{chap:state-of-the-art}

\begin{introduction}
This chapter reviews existing work relevant to the viewer developed in this thesis. It begins with the digital twin concept and its application to physical infrastructure, then surveys the shift from dedicated Geographic Information System (GIS) tooling to real-time game engines for geospatial visualisation, covers the handling of simulated entities alongside physical ones, and finally examines comparable viewer systems and how tightly each couples its rendering to a particular backend and data model. The chapter closes by stating the gap this thesis targets.
\end{introduction}

\section{The Digital Twin Concept}
\label{sec:dt-concept}

The digital twin concept is generally traced to Michael Grieves, who presented an early form of it in the context of product lifecycle management in the early 2000s, later articulated as a model comprising a physical product, its virtual counterpart, and the data connections binding the two~\cite{grieves2016digitaltwin}. The term itself gained currency after \textcite{glaessgen2012digitaltwin} used it to describe an ultra-high-fidelity simulation of an aerospace vehicle, continuously updated from onboard sensor data and fleet history to mirror the life of its physical twin.

Since then the term has spread across domains far beyond aerospace and manufacturing, and its meaning has broadened accordingly. \textcite{abdelrahman2025whatisdigitaltwin} analysed over 15,000 scientific publications on digital twins in the built environment and found the concept used inconsistently, with definitions varying in how much autonomy, fidelity, and bidirectionality they require. A common core nonetheless recurs: a physical entity, a virtual entity representing it, and a connection between them that is, at minimum, a live flow of data from physical to virtual. Stricter definitions add a return path -- the virtual model influencing the physical system -- and a degree of simulation or prediction.

For the purposes of this thesis, the relevant notion is deliberately modest. The system of interest maintains a virtual representation of physical (and simulated) infrastructure entities, kept current by a live data feed. Whether such a system is a ``true'' digital twin under a stricter definition, and whether prediction or actuation should be added, are questions about the \emph{backend} and are outside the scope set in Section~\ref{sec:scope}. What matters here is that the data feed exists and that something must render it.

\section{Digital Twins for Physical Infrastructure}
\label{sec:dt-infrastructure}

\textcite{broo2021digitaltwins} narrow the concept to infrastructure specifically, describing digital twins in this domain as an emerging idea without a commonly accepted definition, but converging on a realistic digital representation of assets, processes, or systems in the built environment, kept synchronised with their physical counterpart. In the mobility sub-domain, the entities are the components of a road network and the sensing and telematics equipment that instruments them, as enumerated in Section~\ref{sec:context}.

Architecturally, an infrastructure digital twin is conventionally split into a backend that models and serves entity data and a viewer that renders it. The backend's role is to abstract over the heterogeneity of the physical layer -- many device types, many protocols -- and expose a uniform interface. Eclipse Ditto is an open-source middleware built for exactly this: it represents physical devices as digital twins behind a common HTTP and WebSocket API, decoupling client applications from how each device actually communicates~\cite{kherbache2022ditto}. Ditto is explicitly a data layer; it provides no visualisation. \textcite{kherbache2022ditto} pair it with Eclipse Hono for device connectivity and demonstrate the combination as a digital twin network for the industrial IoT, but any three-dimensional or geospatial representation of the twins it manages must be built separately.

This backend/viewer separation is near-universal in the surveyed literature: the system that models the twin's data and the system that renders it are treated, and built, as distinct concerns. Where systems fall short of that separation \emph{within} the viewer -- the viewer's own code being written against one backend and one entity schema -- is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:related-viewer-systems}.

\section{Geospatial Visualisation: From GIS to Game Engines}
\label{sec:geospatial-vis}

Rendering a digital twin of physical infrastructure at real-world scale requires placing content accurately on a geographic reference frame. This has historically been the province of GIS software and of standardised 3D city models: CityGML and its JSON encoding CityJSON describe urban objects -- buildings, roads, vegetation, city furniture -- with defined geometry and semantics, and most urban digital twin platforms are built around such a model.

Real-time 3D game engines have become a practical alternative as they have gained geospatial capabilities. \textcite{rantanen2023opengeospatial} integrate open geospatial data into a game engine for urban digital twin applications and describe the data-conversion pipeline this requires; \textcite{ene2026urbandigitaltwins} survey the combined use of GIS and game engine systems for urban digital twins and find game engines increasingly viable where interactivity and rendering quality matter more than analytical GIS functionality. The trade-off both note is that game engines bring strong rendering and interaction but must be bridged to geospatial data and coordinate systems that they do not natively understand.

The bridge used in this work is Cesium for Unreal~\cite{cesiumforunreal}, a free and open-source plugin that embeds a full-scale WGS84 globe in Unreal Engine and streams terrain, imagery, and 3D content into it. The streaming format is 3D Tiles, an OGC Community Standard for organising and delivering massive heterogeneous 3D geospatial datasets -- point clouds, photogrammetry, BIM and CAD models, 3D buildings -- as a spatially indexed hierarchy suitable for progressive loading~\cite{ogc3dtiles}. Cesium for Unreal was chosen over building geospatial placement from scratch, and over a pure web or GIS viewer, because it retains Unreal Engine's rendering and interaction capabilities -- relevant for a viewer intended to generalise across infrastructure types with potentially rich per-entity visuals -- while still anchoring content to accurate real-world coordinates. Section~\ref{sec:geospatial-placement} in Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} describes how this thesis builds a single coordinate-conversion seam on top of Cesium's georeferencing rather than scattering coordinate mathematics across the application.

\section{Simulated Entities Alongside Physical Ones}
\label{sec:simulated-entities}

A viewer for a mobility digital twin is increasingly expected to display not only physical infrastructure but also entities produced by simulation and streamed through the same backend. Two cases arose in this work. The first is traffic: microscopic traffic simulators such as SUMO model individual vehicles' movement through a road network~\cite{lopez2018sumo}, producing a continuous, high-churn stream of short-lived vehicle entities, each with a position that changes every update. The second is hazard modelling: a wildfire-spread simulation produces a projected burn perimeter and spread cone that evolve over time and are attached to an ignition-point entity.

These entities differ from physical infrastructure in ways that matter to the viewer. They are numerous and ephemeral, they update far more frequently, they may share a single fleet-wide lifetime rather than expiring individually, and their geometry may be delivered as GeoJSON rather than as a mesh. A viewer whose entity handling is specialised to static infrastructure will not accommodate them without change. This reinforces the need, argued throughout this thesis, for an entity model that is generic over what an entity is rather than assuming one shape.

\section{Related Viewer Systems}
\label{sec:related-viewer-systems}

Several existing systems pair a data backend with a geospatial viewer for infrastructure-adjacent digital twins, and are useful comparators for how tightly the two are coupled.

City2Twin~\cite{rafamatanantsoa2024city2twin} presents an open urban digital twin pipeline that integrates municipal open data into a 3D city model for visualisation and analysis. Like most urban digital twin platforms surveyed by \textcite{ene2026urbandigitaltwins}, it is organised around a specific data pipeline and city-model format -- typically CityGML or CityJSON -- and is not designed to be repointed at an arbitrary IoT backend or entity schema. The 3D city model is both its input and its internal representation.

Cesium for Unreal itself, and the wider Cesium platform, are backend-agnostic in principle: they render whatever geospatial data they are given. But they ship no entity model, no live-update mechanism, and no interaction layer for IoT-style digital twins. Applications built on Cesium -- including those surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:geospatial-vis} -- still implement their own data layer, and that layer is typically written for one project's backend and one project's entity types. The geospatial foundation is reusable; the digital twin logic on top of it generally is not.

Across these systems a common pattern appears: the viewer's rendering and interaction code is written against one backend's data model, one fixed set of entity types, or both. This is a defensible choice for a single-deployment viewer, but it means the viewer cannot be reused for a different infrastructure domain or a different backend without editing its core, because there is no seam separating ``what an entity looks like and does'' or ``where its data comes from'' from the rest of the application.

\section{Summary and Research Gaps}
\label{sec:research-gaps}

The literature establishes the digital twin as a physical entity, a virtual representation, and a live connection between them, and establishes infrastructure digital twins as a backend/viewer split in which the backend (for example Eclipse Ditto) models entity data and the viewer renders it. It shows that game engines paired with geospatial plugins such as Cesium for Unreal, streaming standardised formats such as 3D Tiles, are now a viable alternative to GIS-specific tooling for building that viewer. It also shows that such viewers are increasingly expected to handle simulated entities -- traffic, hazards -- alongside physical infrastructure.

What the surveyed viewer systems do not generally address is generalisation and decoupling \emph{within} the viewer. Existing systems tend to fix both the entity types they can display and the backend they fetch from, so neither can change without changing the viewer's core code, and the viewer cannot run without a live instance of its specific backend. This thesis targets that gap: it presents a viewer whose entity model and data-access layer are each a single replaceable seam rather than assumptions baked throughout the rendering and interaction code, as detailed in Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture}.
